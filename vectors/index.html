How the topic fits together

Vectors in IB are one idea used three ways. A vector is a movement, so you can add, scale and measure it. The two products give you angles (dot) and areas and normals (cross). Then lines and planes are built from points and directions. Work through in order, or jump to a weak spot.

AHL 3.12

Vector basics

Vectors describe movement from one place to another. This page covers position vectors, displacement, length, unit vectors, and dividing a line in a ratio.

The idea

A position vector a = OA is the journey from the origin to A. The journey from A to B is AB = b − a: end minus start. Every other result in this unit is built on that one rule.

|v| is Pythagoras in 3D, applied twice. A unit vector has the same direction but length 1, so divide by the length.

To find P on AB with AP : PB = m : n, walk m/(m + n) of the way from A to B. The midpoint is the case m = n. Three points are collinear when AB is a scalar multiple of AC.

The live model is drawn in 2D so you can see everything at once. The algebra for three components is identical, with one more term.

Formulas

|v| = √(v₁² + v₂² + v₃²)booklet
AB = b − a
unit vector = v ÷ |v|
midpoint = ½(a + b)
p = a + (m ÷ (m + n)) AB, for AP : PB = m : n
collinear: AB = λ AC
Traps
  • AB and BA are opposites: AB = −BA.
  • "AP : PB = 1 : 2" means one third of the way, not a half.
  • Squaring first: |kv| = |k||v|, so a negative k does not give a negative length.
Shorthand
  • End minus start. Always AB = b − a.
  • Ratio m : n → fraction m/(m + n).

AHL 3.13

Scalar product

The scalar product turns two vectors into one number that tells you how much they point the same way.

The idea

Two formulas for the same number: a·b = a₁b₁ + a₂b₂ + a₃b₃ and a·b = |a||b| cos θ. The first one is how you calculate. The second one is why it measures angle. Set them equal and you get the angle formula.

Because cos is positive for acute angles, zero at 90° and negative for obtuse ones, the sign of the dot product tells you the shape of the angle straight away. Zero means perpendicular.

The scalar projection a·b ÷ |b| is the length of the shadow of a along b. It is positive if the arrows lean the same way and negative if they lean opposite.

For an angle in a triangle, both vectors must start at the vertex: the angle at B uses BA and BC.

Formulas

a·b = a₁b₁ + a₂b₂ + a₃b₃booklet
a·b = |a||b| cos θbooklet
cos θ = a·b ÷ (|a||b|)booklet
a·a = |a|²
perpendicular ⇔ a·b = 0
scalar projection of a on b = a·b ÷ |b|
Traps
  • The dot product is a number. A vector answer loses the mark.
  • The angle at B uses BA·BC, not AB·BC. AB points the wrong way.
  • (a + b)·(a + b) = |a|² + 2a·b + |b|², not |a|² + |b|².
Shorthand
  • Perpendicular? Dot = 0.
  • Angle at a vertex: both arrows start there.

AHL 3.15

Vector product

The vector product takes two vectors and produces a third, perpendicular to both. Its size is an area.

The idea

a × b is a vector that is perpendicular to both a and b, pointing the way a right-handed screw would move turning from a to b. Its length is the area of the parallelogram the two vectors span: |a||b| sin θ.

The dot product has cos, the cross product has sin. When the vectors are parallel, sin θ = 0, so the cross product is zero. That is the test for parallel.

Two jobs come up constantly. The cross product of two directions in a plane gives the normal to the plane. Half its magnitude gives the area of a triangle.

Rotate the model, then drag v to be parallel to u and watch the parallelogram collapse and u × v shrink to zero.

Formulas

a × b = (a₂b₃ − a₃b₂, a₃b₁ − a₁b₃, a₁b₂ − a₂b₁)booklet
|a × b| = |a||b| sin θbooklet
parallelogram area = |a × b|booklet
triangle ABC area = ½|AB × AC|
b × a = −(a × b)
a × b = 0 ⇔ a ∥ b
Traps
  • Order matters: b × a points the opposite way.
  • Triangle area needs the ½: the cross product gives the parallelogram.
  • Do not mix up: dot product uses cos, cross product uses sin.
Shorthand
  • Dot uses cos, cross uses sin.
  • Check: dot the result with each input. Both must be 0.

AHL 3.14

Lines

A line in 3D needs a point and a direction. There is no y = mx + c, because a line in space has no single gradient.

The idea

r = a + λb: start at the point A, then move any multiple λ of the direction b. Different λ give different points on the same line. The direction is usually the vector AB between two known points, and any multiple of it describes the same line.

To check that a point is on the line, find λ from one component, then confirm the other two. To get the Cartesian form, make λ the subject of each component and set them equal.

The angle between two lines is the angle between their directions (taken acute). The shortest distance from a point P to the line is the length of the perpendicular PF, equal to |AP × b| ÷ |b|: the parallelogram area divided by its base.

For a moving particle, b is the velocity, |b| is the speed, and the position at time t is a + tb.

Formulas

r = a + λbbooklet
(x − x₀) ÷ l = (y − y₀) ÷ m = (z − z₀) ÷ nbooklet
cos θ = |b₁·b₂| ÷ (|b₁||b₂|)
distance from P to line = |AP × b| ÷ |b|
closest approach: AP·b = λ|b|²
speed = |b|
Traps
  • To show a point is on the line, check all three components.
  • A zero in the direction vector: write y = constant instead of dividing by 0.
  • The angle between lines uses only directions. The points are decoys.
Shorthand
  • Point + direction.
  • On the line? One λ for all three components.

AHL 3.14

Two lines

In 2D, lines that are not parallel always meet. In 3D they can pass without touching: parallel, intersecting, coincident or skew.

The idea

Start with the directions. If one is a multiple of the other, the lines are parallel or the same line: put the point A₂ into line 1 to tell which.

If the directions are not parallel, equate the lines. This gives three equations in two unknowns λ and μ. Solve any two, then test the third. If it holds, the lines intersect there. If it fails, there is no solution and the lines are skew.

Skew lines have a shortest distance, along the direction perpendicular to both: n = b₁ × b₂. Project the connecting vector a₂ − a₁ onto n.

In the model, line 1 is fixed. Press a preset, then move line 2 and watch the verdict change. Rotate to see why skew lines look like they cross from some angles.

Formulas

a₁ + λb₁ = a₂ + μb₂
parallel: b₂ = kb₁
skew gap = |(a₂ − a₁)·(b₁ × b₂)| ÷ |b₁ × b₂|
cos θ = |b₁·b₂| ÷ (|b₁||b₂|)
Traps
  • Use different letters, λ and μ, for the two lines.
  • Skew needs both facts: not parallel, and no intersection.
  • Matching the third equation is part of the proof, not a bonus.
Shorthand
  • Parallel? Same line? Else solve two, test the third.
  • Third fails → skew.

AHL 3.16

Planes

A plane is pinned down by a point and a normal, a vector sticking out at right angles.

The idea

Every vector lying in the plane is perpendicular to the normal n. So for a point r on the plane through A, (r − a)·n = 0, which rearranges to r·n = a·n. That constant is d. This is the whole idea.

A plane has three descriptions. Vector form r = a + λb + μc gives a point and two directions. Normal form r·n = d. Cartesian form ax + by + cz = d, where (a, b, c) is the normal. To convert, cross the two directions for n, then dot with the point for d.

The distance from P to the plane is the shadow of AP along n: |n·p − d| ÷ |n|.

Drag d and the normal in the model. The green arrow is always at right angles to the plane.

Formulas

r = a + λb + μcbooklet
r·n = a·nbooklet
ax + by + cz = dbooklet
n = b × c, or AB × AC for three points
distance from P to plane = |n·p − d| ÷ |n|
distance from the origin = |d| ÷ |n|
Traps
  • n = b × c uses the two directions, never the point.
  • Check your plane with the other points on it.
  • Scaling n changes d too: 2x + 2y + 2z = 6 is the same plane as x + y + z = 3.
Shorthand
  • Normal first (cross), then d (dot).
  • Distance: plug in, subtract d, divide by |n|.

AHL 3.16

Line and plane

A line and a plane can meet at one point, never meet, or the line can lie inside the plane.

The idea

Write the line as x = a₁ + λb₁, y = a₂ + λb₂, z = a₃ + λb₃ and substitute into the plane equation. Everything collapses to one linear equation in λ: n·a + λ(n·b) = d.

Three outcomes. If n·b ≠ 0 you get one value of λ, hence one point. If n·b = 0 the line is parallel to the plane: either a·n ≠ d (never meets) or a·n = d (the line lies in the plane).

The angle between a line and a plane is measured against the plane, not the normal. Since the normal is at 90° to the plane, the angle with the plane satisfies sin θ = |b·n| ÷ (|b||n|).

To reflect P in a plane, find the foot of the perpendicular F by sending a line along the normal, then go the same distance again: P′ = 2F − P.

Formulas

n·a + λ(n·b) = d
parallel to the plane ⇔ b·n = 0
sin θ = |b·n| ÷ (|b||n|)
foot of the perpendicular: line through P along n
reflection P′ = 2F − P
Traps
  • The angle with the plane uses sin, not cos.
  • b·n = 0 alone does not tell you the line misses the plane. Check a·n against d.
  • Put λ back into the LINE, not the plane, to get the point.
Shorthand
  • Line into plane → one equation in λ.
  • b·n = 0 → parallel. Then a·n vs d.

AHL 3.16

Planes meeting

Two planes meet in a line. Three planes can meet at a point, along a line, or not at all.

The idea

Two planes are parallel exactly when their normals are. Otherwise they meet in a line whose direction is n₁ × n₂, perpendicular to both normals. The angle between the planes is the angle between the normals.

For three planes, write the equations as a system of three equations in x, y, z. If the determinant of the coefficients is non-zero, there is exactly one solution and the planes meet at a point. If the determinant is zero, the planes either share a line or share nothing.

When there is no solution, say which of two pictures it is: a triangular prism (every pair meets in a line, but the three lines are parallel and no point is common), or two parallel planes cut by a third. In the prism all three normals lie in a flat sheet, which is why the determinant is zero.

PictureNormals / determinantSolutions
Single pointdet ≠ 0exactly one
Common linedet = 0, consistentinfinitely many
Triangular prismdet = 0, no parallel pairnone
Two parallel planesdet = 0, two normals parallelnone

Formulas

direction of intersection = n₁ × n₂
cos θ = |n₁·n₂| ÷ (|n₁||n₂|)
det ≠ 0 ⇔ unique solution
det = n₃·(n₁ × n₂)
Traps
  • The right-hand sides d never affect the angle or the direction.
  • No solution does not mean parallel: a prism has no parallel planes.
  • Always finish with a sentence naming the geometry.
Shorthand
  • det ≠ 0: a point. det = 0: eliminate and look at the last line.
  • 0 = 0 → a line. 0 = 5 → no common point.

Mixed

Mixed drill

Questions from every vector topic in random order. Part of the skill is recognising which tool a question wants before you start.

Exam habits that save marks

  • Write vectors as columns with brackets and keep the letters: "AB = b − a".
  • For "show that", write every step, including the check that the third equation holds.
  • Set the calculator to degrees before any angle. Keep full precision until the last step.
  • For a plane or line, check your final equation with a second known point.
  • Name the geometry in words at the end: "the lines are skew", "the planes form a triangular prism".
