How calculus fits together

Calculus has two halves joined at the middle. Differentiation finds the rate of change at a point: the gradient of the tangent. Integration adds up thin strips: an area, a volume, a distance. The fundamental theorem says each undoes the other. Every topic here is one of those two ideas applied to a different question, and most of the exam skill is recognising which one a question wants.

SL 5.1 · AHL 5.12

Limits, continuity and first principles

The derivative is the limit of chord gradients as the second point slides into the first. Continuity and differentiability are about whether that limit exists.

The idea

A limit is the value a function approaches, not necessarily the value it takes. (x² − 9) ÷ (x − 3) is undefined at x = 3, but it equals x + 3 everywhere else, so its limit at 3 is 6. When substitution gives 0/0, there is a common factor to cancel.

The gradient of the chord from (a, f(a)) to (a + h, f(a + h)) is (f(a + h) − f(a)) ÷ h. As h shrinks the chord swings round into the tangent, so the derivative is defined as f′(a) = limh→0 (f(a + h) − f(a)) ÷ h. "From first principles" means using this definition: expand, cancel the f(a) terms, divide by h, then let h → 0.

f is continuous at a if you can draw through x = a without lifting the pen: f(a) exists and the limits from both sides equal it. f is differentiable at a if the chord gradient has the same limit from both sides. Differentiable implies continuous, but not the other way: |x| is continuous at 0 with a corner, so the left and right gradients are −1 and 1.

In the model, slide h towards 0 from both sides and watch the blue chord turn into the green tangent. Pick |x| with a = 0 to see the two one-sided chords refuse to agree.

Formulas

f′(x) = limh→0 (f(x + h) − f(x)) ÷ hbooklet
chord gradient = (f(a + h) − f(a)) ÷ h
continuous at a: limx→a⁻ f = limx→a⁺ f = f(a)
differentiable at a: continuous, and left and right gradients agree
(a + h)³ = a³ + 3a²h + 3ah² + h³
Traps
  • Using the power rule when "first principles" is asked scores 0.
  • Divide by h before letting h → 0, never after.
  • Matching gradients is not enough; check continuity too.
  • A calculator's d/dx at a corner gives an average, not a derivative.
Shorthand
  • Expand, cancel, divide by h, then h → 0.
  • 0/0 means a common factor: factorise.
  • Joins: values match (continuous), slopes match (differentiable).

AHL 5.13

L'Hôpital's rule

When a limit comes out as 0/0 or ∞/∞, compare how fast the top and bottom are changing instead of what they are.

The idea

Near x = a, a differentiable function looks like its tangent: f(x) ≈ f(a) + f′(a)(x − a). If f(a) = g(a) = 0, then f(x) ÷ g(x) ≈ f′(a)(x − a) ÷ (g′(a)(x − a)) = f′(a) ÷ g′(a). The (x − a) cancels. That is l'Hôpital's rule: for 0/0 (or ∞/∞), lim f/g = lim f′/g′.

Differentiate the top and bottom separately. It is not the quotient rule. If the new fraction is still 0/0, apply the rule again, checking the form each time.

Other forms must be rewritten first. 0 × ∞, like x ln x as x → 0⁺, becomes a fraction: ln x ÷ (1/x). Powers like xˣ become ex ln x: find the limit of the exponent, then exponentiate.

In the model, the blue top and amber bottom both pass through 0 at x = a, but their ratio (green) heads smoothly to a finite value. Move x towards a and watch the ratio settle on f′(a) ÷ g′(a).

Formulas

0/0 or ∞/∞: lim f(x)/g(x) = lim f′(x)/g′(x)
limx→0 sin x ÷ x = 1
limx→0 (eˣ − 1) ÷ x = 1
limx→0 (1 − cos x) ÷ x² = ½
0 × ∞: rewrite as a fraction; 0⁰, 1^∞: take ln
Traps
  • Check the form every time. On a non-0/0 fraction the rule gives wrong answers.
  • Top′ over bottom′, never the quotient rule.
  • x ln x is 0 × ∞: rewrite before applying.
  • Radians: d/dx sin x = cos x only in radians.
Shorthand
  • Substitute first. 0/0? Differentiate top and bottom.
  • Still 0/0? Go again.
  • ln on top: x ln x = ln x ÷ (1/x).

SL 5.3, 5.6 · AHL 5.15

Differentiation rules

Most functions are built from simple ones by multiplying, dividing or nesting. Each way of building has its own rule.

The idea

The chain rule handles a function inside a function. If y = f(u) and u = g(x), a small change in x changes u by about g′(x) times as much, which changes y by about f′(u) times that. So dy/dx = dy/du × du/dx: outer derivative (inner left alone) times inner derivative.

The product rule, (uv)′ = u′v + uv′, comes from the area of a rectangle with sides u and v. Grow both sides a little: the new area is the old plus a strip v du plus a strip u dv (plus a tiny corner that vanishes in the limit).

The quotient rule, (u/v)′ = (u′v − uv′) ÷ v², is the product rule applied to u × v⁻¹. Order matters in the numerator because of the minus sign.

The HL booklet adds derivatives of tan, sec, cosec, cot, aˣ, logₐ x, arcsin, arccos and arctan. Each is used with the chain rule exactly like sin and eˣ. In the model, the dashed amber curve is f′. Its height at any x equals the slope of the blue curve there.

Formulas

(uv)′ = u′v + uv′booklet
(u/v)′ = (u′v − uv′) ÷ v²booklet
dy/dx = dy/du × du/dxbooklet
d/dx tan x = sec² x, d/dx sec x = sec x tan xbooklet
d/dx aˣ = aˣ ln a, d/dx logₐ x = 1/(x ln a)booklet
d/dx arcsin x = 1/√(1 − x²), d/dx arctan x = 1/(1 + x²)booklet
Traps
  • Chain rule: multiply by the inner derivative; it is easy to forget the "× 2" in sin 2x.
  • Quotient rule: u′v − uv′, in that order.
  • d/dx eᵏˣ = keᵏˣ, not xeᵏˣ⁻¹.
  • Radians for every trig derivative.
Shorthand
  • Outer′(inner) × inner′.
  • Low d-high minus high d-low, over low squared.
  • Factorise at the end: e^x and brackets come out.

SL 5.2, 5.4

Tangents, normals, increasing and decreasing

The derivative at a point is the gradient of the tangent there. Its sign says whether the function is going up or down.

The idea

The tangent at x = a touches the curve and has gradient m = f′(a). It passes through (a, f(a)), so its equation is y − f(a) = f′(a)(x − a). The point comes from f, the gradient from f′: two different functions, one line.

The normal is perpendicular to the tangent at the same point. Perpendicular gradients multiply to −1, so the normal has gradient −1/f′(a). If the tangent is horizontal, the normal is the vertical line x = a.

f is increasing where f′(x) > 0 and decreasing where f′(x) < 0. To find the intervals, solve f′(x) = 0 and test the sign of f′ between the roots. In the model the curve is drawn green where it rises and red where it falls.

A line y = mx + c is tangent to a quadratic curve when they meet exactly once: substitute, and set the discriminant to zero. The model keeps equal scales on both axes so the normal really looks perpendicular.

Formulas

tangent: y − y₁ = m(x − x₁), m = f′(x₁)
normal gradient = −1 ÷ m
perpendicular: m₁m₂ = −1
increasing ⇔ f′(x) > 0, decreasing ⇔ f′(x) < 0
tangent to a quadratic: discriminant b² − 4ac = 0
Traps
  • The y-coordinate comes from f, not f′.
  • Normal: flip AND change sign.
  • "Increasing" questions want an interval of x, not a y-value.
  • Parallel tangents have equal gradients: solve f′(x) = m.
Shorthand
  • Gradient from f′, point from f.
  • Normal = −1 ÷ tangent gradient.
  • Rising = f′ positive.

SL 5.7, 5.8

Stationary points, concavity and inflexion

Line up the graphs of f, f′ and f″ and every feature of one shows up as a feature of the next.

The idea

A stationary point has f′(x) = 0: the tangent is horizontal. It is a maximum if f′ goes from + to −, a minimum if − to +, and a stationary inflexion if f′ keeps the same sign either side.

f″ measures how the gradient is changing, which is the concavity. f″ > 0: the gradient is increasing, the curve bends upwards (concave up), so a stationary point there is a minimum. f″ < 0: concave down, a maximum. If f″ = 0, the second-derivative test says nothing; go back to the sign of f′.

A point of inflexion is where the concavity changes: f″ = 0 and f″ changes sign. If f′ is also 0 there, it is a stationary inflexion; otherwise it is non-stationary. x⁴ has f″(0) = 0 but no inflexion, because f″ doesn't change sign.

In the model, the three graphs share one x-axis. Roots of f′ (filled dots) line up with the turning points of f. Roots of f″ with a sign change (rings) line up with the inflexions of f and the turning points of f′. Green shading marks where f′ or f″ is positive.

Formulas

stationary: f′(x) = 0
f″ > 0 ⇒ minimum, f″ < 0 ⇒ maximum
f″ = 0 at a stationary point ⇒ use the sign of f′ either side
inflexion: f″ = 0 and f″ changes sign
concave up ⇔ f″ > 0
Traps
  • f″ = 0 alone is not an inflexion: show the sign change.
  • Find y from f, not f′.
  • A stationary point with f″ = 0 can be a max, a min or an inflexion.
  • eˣ factors are never 0: say so, then ignore them.
Shorthand
  • f′ = 0 finds them, f″ sorts them.
  • Inflexion = f″ changes sign.
  • Turning points of f′ are inflexions of f.

SL 5.8 · AHL 5.14

Optimisation

Write the quantity you want to make biggest or smallest as a function of one variable, then find where its derivative is zero.

The idea

Optimisation problems hide a function inside a story. The work is setting it up: name the quantity to optimise, write it in terms of two dimensions, then use the constraint (a fixed volume, perimeter or sum) to replace one of them. Now it is a function of one variable.

At a maximum or minimum inside the allowed range, the tangent is horizontal, so set the derivative to zero. Then justify the nature: the sign of the second derivative, or the sign of the first derivative either side.

Watch the domain. A box with cut x needs 0 < x < L/2; a root outside that range (or one that gives zero volume) is rejected. If the derivative is never zero inside the range, the optimum is at an endpoint.

In the model, slide your choice of dimension along the allowed range. The amber line is the tangent to the objective: flat only at the optimum.

Formulas

optimum where f′(x) = 0 (inside the domain)
f″ < 0 ⇒ maximum, f″ > 0 ⇒ minimum
cylinder: V = πr²h, S = 2πr² + 2πrh
open box from an L × L square: V = x(L − 2x)²
minimise distance: minimise D²
Traps
  • Use the constraint to get ONE variable before differentiating.
  • Reject roots outside the domain or giving a zero dimension.
  • Justify max or min; f′ = 0 alone is not enough.
  • Answer the question: the value, the dimension, or both.
Shorthand
  • Name it, constrain it, differentiate it, justify it.
  • Distance? Minimise D².
  • No zero inside? Check the endpoints.

AHL 5.14

Implicit differentiation and related rates

Differentiate an equation in x and y term by term, treating y as a function of x. The same chain rule links rates that change together in time.

The idea

Many curves, like x² + y² = 25, are not written as y = f(x). You can still differentiate: treat y as an unknown function of x, and differentiate both sides with respect to x. By the chain rule, d/dx (y²) = 2y dy/dx. Every y-term picks up a dy/dx. A term like xy needs the product rule: y + x dy/dx.

Then collect the dy/dx terms, factor and divide. The answer usually contains both x and y, which is fine: a curve like a circle has two points at each x, with different gradients.

Related rates use the same idea with time. If a balloon's volume and radius both change, V = (4/3)πr³ gives dV/dt = 4πr² dr/dt. Write the geometric formula, differentiate with respect to t, and only then substitute the values at the instant asked.

In the model, slide the point around the curve. The tangent is horizontal where the numerator of dy/dx is 0 and vertical where the denominator is 0.

Formulas

d/dx (yⁿ) = nyⁿ⁻¹ dy/dx
d/dx (xy) = y + x dy/dx
dy/dx = (dy/dt) ÷ (dx/dt)
dV/dt = dV/dr × dr/dt
sphere V = (4/3)πr³, cone V = (1/3)πr²h
Traps
  • Forgetting dy/dx on a y-term.
  • xy needs the product rule.
  • Substitute values AFTER differentiating, never before.
  • Cones: remove r with similar triangles before differentiating.
Shorthand
  • Every y gets a dy/dx.
  • Formula → d/dt → substitute.
  • Horizontal: top = 0. Vertical: bottom = 0.

SL 5.5, 5.10, 5.11

Integration and the fundamental theorem

Integration undoes differentiation, and a definite integral adds up thin strips to give the signed area under a curve.

The idea

An antiderivative of f is any F with F′ = f. Because constants differentiate to 0, there is a whole family: ∫ f(x) dx = F(x) + c. Any point on the curve fixes c.

A definite integral is the limit of a sum of thin rectangles: height f(x), width dx. The fundamental theorem of calculus says that this sum equals F(b) − F(a) for any antiderivative F. That is why "integrate, upper minus lower" gives an area.

Area below the x-axis counts as negative. If the curve crosses the axis, the signed integral and the total area differ; the Areas topic deals with this.

Reverse chain rule: if the integrand is f′(x) × g(f(x)), the answer is G(f(x)). Guess the answer, differentiate it, and adjust the constant. In the model, raise n and watch the rectangle sum close in on F(b) − F(a).

Formulas

∫ xⁿ dx = xⁿ⁺¹ ÷ (n + 1) + c, n ≠ −1booklet
∫ 1/x dx = ln|x| + cbooklet
∫ eᵏˣ dx = eᵏˣ ÷ k + c
∫ sin x dx = −cos x + c, ∫ cos x dx = sin x + cbooklet
∫ from a to b of f(x) dx = F(b) − F(a)
∫ f′(x)(f(x))ⁿ dx = (f(x))ⁿ⁺¹ ÷ (n + 1) + c
Traps
  • + c on every indefinite integral.
  • ∫ 1/x dx is ln|x|, not x⁰/0.
  • eᵏˣ: divide by k, don't multiply.
  • e⁰ = 1, not 0, at a lower limit of 0.
Shorthand
  • Up one, divide by the new power.
  • Upper minus lower.
  • Guess, differentiate, adjust.

Integration techniques: parts, substitution, partial fractions

When an integral isn't standard, rewrite it until it is: undo a product rule, change the variable, or split the fraction.

The idea

Integration by parts undoes the product rule. From (uv)′ = u′v + uv′, integrating gives ∫ u dv = uv − ∫ v du. Choose u to be the factor that gets simpler when differentiated: Logs, Inverse trig, Algebraic, Trig, Exponential (LIATE), earliest first. Sometimes parts twice brings the original integral back: then solve for it as an unknown, as with ∫ eˣ sin x dx.

The model draws parts as areas. Plot v against u: the region under the curve is ∫ v du, the region to its left is ∫ u dv, and together they fill the rectangle uv. That is the whole formula in one picture.

Substitution undoes the chain rule. Replace an inner function by u, replace dx using du = u′(x) dx, and change the limits to u-values. If a stray x is left over, write it in terms of u.

Partial fractions split (px + q) ÷ ((x − a)(x − b)) into A/(x − a) + B/(x − b), each of which integrates to a log. Find A and B by putting x = a and x = b (the cover-up method). Integrals with a² + x² or √(a² − x²) are booklet arctan and arcsin forms.

∫ u dv = uv − ∫ v dubooklet
∫ 1/(a² + x²) dx = (1/a) arctan(x/a) + cbooklet
∫ 1/√(a² − x²) dx = arcsin(x/a) + cbooklet
∫ f′(x)/f(x) dx = ln|f(x)| + c
(px + q)/((x − a)(x − b)) = A/(x − a) + B/(x − b)
Traps
  • By parts: u = the polynomial with eˣ or trig, but u = ln x whenever ln x appears.
  • Substitution: change the limits, or go back to x before substituting old ones.
  • Partial fractions need a factorised denominator of higher degree.
  • eˣ sin x by parts twice: keep u as the trig both times.
Shorthand
  • LIATE picks u.
  • Substitute everything: dx, limits, leftover x.

Kinematics

Displacement, velocity and acceleration are one chain: differentiate to go down it, integrate to come back up.

The idea

For motion along a line, v = ds/dt and a = dv/dt. Going the other way, s = ∫ v dt and v = ∫ a dt, each with a constant fixed by an initial condition.

Displacement is where you end up relative to where you started: ∫ v dt, signs and all. Distance is how far you actually travelled: ∫ |v| dt. They differ whenever the particle turns round, which happens where v = 0 and changes sign.

A particle is at rest when v = 0. It is speeding up when v and a have the same sign, and slowing down when they have opposite signs. "Speed" is |v|.

In the model, the shaded areas under the velocity graph are the displacements: green forward, red back. The strip below shows the particle moving along the line, turning where v crosses zero.

Formulas

displacement = ∫ from t₁ to t₂ of v dtbooklet
distance = ∫ from t₁ to t₂ of |v| dtbooklet
at rest: v = 0
speeding up: v and a have the same sign
Traps
  • Distance needs |v|: split where v = 0, or use abs on the GDC.
  • "At rest" is v = 0, not a = 0.
  • Two integrations, two constants: use v(0) then s(0).
  • Maximum velocity is where a = 0.
Shorthand
  • s → v → a: differentiate.
  • Distance = area, all positive.
  • Same signs speed up.

Maclaurin series

Build a polynomial that matches a function's value, slope, curvature and so on at x = 0. Near 0 it is an excellent stand-in for the function.

The idea

A polynomial a₀ + a₁x + a₂x² + … has a₀ = its value at 0, a₁ = its first derivative at 0, 2!a₂ = its second derivative at 0, and so on. To make it match f at 0 to every order, choose aₖ = f⁽ᵏ⁾(0) ÷ k!. That gives the Maclaurin series f(x) = f(0) + f′(0)x + f″(0)x²/2! + …

In practice you rarely differentiate from scratch. Start from a booklet series and substitute (e2x: put 2x into eˣ), multiply two series and collect powers, or differentiate or integrate term by term.

Some series work for every x (eˣ, sin x, cos x). Others only converge on an interval: ln(1 + x) needs −1 < x ≤ 1, and 1/(1 − x) needs |x| < 1. Outside it, adding terms makes things worse.

Series also give limits (expand, cancel, divide) and integrals of functions with no antiderivative, like e−x². In the model, raise n and watch the blue polynomial hug the green curve over a wider range; for ln(1 + x), watch it peel away outside the shaded interval.

Formulas

f(x) = f(0) + f′(0)x + f″(0)x²/2! + …booklet
eˣ = 1 + x + x²/2! + x³/3! + …booklet
sin x = x − x³/3! + x⁵/5! − …, cos x = 1 − x²/2! + x⁴/4! − …booklet
ln(1 + x) = x − x²/2 + x³/3 − …, −1 < x ≤ 1booklet
(1 + x)ᵖ = 1 + px + p(p − 1)x²/2! + …, |x| < 1booklet
arctan x = x − x³/3 + x⁵/5 − …booklet
Traps
  • Divide by k!, not k.
  • Substituting 2x: every x becomes (2x), so x² becomes 4x².
  • Products: collect ALL pairs of powers that add to the target.
  • Outside the interval of convergence the series is useless.
Shorthand
  • Substitute into a known series first.
  • Products: list the pairs of powers.
  • Limits: expand, cancel, divide, x → 0.

Modelling and Euler's method

Turn a sentence about a rate into an equation, then estimate its solution by following the slope one small step at a time.

The idea

Modelling. "The rate of change of y" is dy/dt. "Proportional to" means "equals a constant k times". So "the population grows at a rate proportional to its size" is dP/dt = kP. Newton's law of cooling, "the temperature falls at a rate proportional to the difference from room temperature", is dT/dt = −k(T − Tₐ) with k > 0. The minus sign carries the word "falls". Find k from one piece of data, usually an initial rate.

Euler's method. The equation dy/dx = f(x, y) gives the slope at any point. Stand at (x₀, y₀), work out the slope there, and walk along that tangent for a short horizontal distance h. That lands you at a new point. Re-aim using the slope at the new point and repeat. Each step is new = old + h × slope at old.

The tangent drifts off a curved solution, so Euler has an error. If the solution is concave up the tangent lies below it and Euler underestimates. If concave down, it overestimates. Halving h roughly halves the error. To check concavity, differentiate the differential equation to get d²y/dx².

Slope fields (the grid of short lines in the model) are not examined in AA, but they show exactly what Euler's method is doing.

Formulas

yn+1 = yn + h × f(xn, yn)booklet
xn+1 = xn + hbooklet
number of steps = (xend − x₀) ÷ h
proportional to y: dy/dt = ky
cooling: dT/dt = −k(T − Tₐ), k > 0
limited growth: dP/dt = kP(N − P)
Traps
  • Use the slope at the old point. Update x and y only after computing the slope.
  • Count the steps: from x = 0 to x = 1 with h = 0.25 is four steps, not five.
  • "Decreasing at a rate…" means dy/dt is negative.
  • Keep full calculator values between steps. Round only the final answer.
Shorthand
  • New = old + h × slope at old.
  • Concave up → Euler under. Concave down → Euler over.
  • "Proportional to" = "k ×".

Separation of variables

When dy/dx is a function of x times a function of y, move each variable to its own side and integrate both sides.

The idea

If dy/dx = g(x) h(y), divide by h(y): (1/h(y)) dy/dx = g(x). The left side is the derivative of ∫(1/h(y)) dy with respect to x, by the chain rule. So integrating both sides with respect to x gives ∫ dy/h(y) = ∫ g(x) dx + c. Writing "dy" and "dx" on separate sides is shorthand for that chain-rule step.

You get one constant, from one integral. That gives a whole family of curves (the general solution). A starting value, called an initial condition, picks out one member (the particular solution). Substitute it as soon as c appears, before rearranging. It is usually easier.

dy/dx = ky separates to ln|y| = kx + c, so y = ±ecekx = Aekx. The constant changes shape when you exponentiate: ekx + c is Aekx, never ekx + c.

Dividing by h(y) assumes h(y) ≠ 0. A value with h(y) = 0, like y = 0 in dy/dx = ky, is a constant solution that the division loses. Set y₀ = 0 in the model to see it.

Formulas

∫ dy ÷ h(y) = ∫ g(x) dx + c
∫ dy ÷ y = ln|y| + cbooklet
dy/dx = ky ⇒ y = Aekx
dT/dt = −k(T − Tₐ) ⇒ T = Tₐ + (T₀ − Tₐ)e−kt
1 ÷ (P(N − P)) = (1/N)(1/P + 1/(N − P))
Traps
  • One constant only. Two constants (one per side) combine into one.
  • ex + c = Aex, not ex + c.
  • Forgetting c until after rearranging loses the marks for the particular solution.
  • Write ln|y| unless you know y > 0.
Shorthand
  • y's with dy, x's with dx, one + c.
  • Use the initial condition the moment c appears.

Homogeneous equations: y = vx

When dy/dx depends only on the ratio y/x, the substitution y = vx turns the equation into one you can separate.

The idea

An equation is homogeneous if dy/dx can be written as a function of y/x alone: dy/dx = F(y/x). The test: divide the top and bottom by the highest power of x. If every x and y pairs up into y/x, it is homogeneous. This happens when every term has the same total degree, like x², xy and y².

Geometrically, the slope is the same at every point on a line through the origin, because y/x is constant along it. Slide the ray in the model and watch every slope segment on it line up.

So name the ratio: v = y/x, which means y = vx. Differentiate with the product rule: dy/dx = v + x dv/dx. Substituting gives v + x dv/dx = F(v), so x dv/dx = F(v) − v. That separates: ∫ dv ÷ (F(v) − v) = ∫ dx ÷ x = ln|x| + c.

The answer is in v. Replace v by y/x at the end, and use the initial condition in either form.

Formulas

dy/dx = F(y/x)
y = vx ⇒ dy/dx = v + x dv/dx
x dv/dx = F(v) − v
∫ dv ÷ (F(v) − v) = ln|x| + c
∫ f′(v) ÷ f(v) dv = ln|f(v)| + c
Traps
  • dy/dx = v + x dv/dx, not just dv/dx. The product rule is the whole point.
  • Finishing in v loses the last mark. Put v = y/x back.
  • Subtract v from F(v) before separating: x dv/dx = F(v) − v.
Shorthand
  • Divide by x until only y/x is left.
  • y = vx, dy/dx = v + xv′. Separate. Put y/x back.

AHL 5.18

Integrating factor

For linear equations dy/dx + P(x)y = Q(x), multiply through by a factor that turns the left side into one derivative.

The idea

The product rule says d/dx(I y) = I dy/dx + I′ y. Compare it with I dy/dx + I P y, the left side after multiplying the equation by I. They are the same when I′ = IP, and the function that does that is I = e∫P dx. That is the integrating factor.

With it, the equation becomes d/dx(I y) = I Q. Integrate once: I y = ∫ I Q dx + c. Then divide by I. The c is divided too, which is why the answer ends with a term like c e−2x or c ÷ x².

The method needs the standard form first: dy/dx with coefficient 1. If the question gives x dy/dx + 2y = x², divide by x before reading off P = 2/x. Logs in the exponent cancel: e2 ln x = x². No constant is needed in ∫P dx; any one integrating factor works.

The model splits the solution into two parts: a steady part that follows Q, and a transient c e−kx set by the starting value. With k > 0 the transient dies away. Make k negative and watch it take over.

Mixed drill

Questions from every calculus topic in random order. Part of the skill is recognising which method a question wants before you start.

Exam habits that save marks

  • Write the rule or set-up line on its own (product rule, π∫y² dx, the separated form). That line usually carries an M1.
  • Justify every maximum, minimum and inflexion. "f′ = 0" alone is never enough.
  • Areas and distances are positive: split at crossings, or use the modulus on the GDC.
  • Put + c in as soon as you integrate, and use the initial condition straight away.
  • For Euler, write a table with columns n, x, y and slope. Keep full precision between rows.
  • For y = vx, show dy/dx = v + x dv/dx explicitly, and finish in x and y.
  • Check a final answer by differentiating it, or with d/dx on the calculator at one point.
