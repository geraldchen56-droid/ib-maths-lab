SL 4.5 – 4.6

Events, Venn diagrams and the addition rule

An event is a set of outcomes. Most questions about two events come down to the four regions of a Venn diagram. Once you know all four, you can answer anything.

The idea

For equally likely outcomes, P(A) = n(A) ÷ n(U): favourable outcomes over all outcomes. Every probability sits between 0 and 1, and the probabilities of all outcomes add to 1. That is why P(A′) = 1 − P(A).

If you add P(A) and P(B), the overlap A ∩ B gets counted twice. Subtract it once and you get the addition rule.

Mutually exclusive events cannot happen together, so the overlap is empty: P(A ∩ B) = 0.

Independent events don't affect each other. Knowing A happened leaves the chance of B unchanged, which gives P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B).

Expected number of times A occurs in n trials is n × P(A). It is a long-run average, so it can be a decimal.

Formulas

P(A′) = 1 − P(A)booklet
P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B)booklet
Mutually exclusive: P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B)booklet
Independent: P(A ∩ B) = P(A)P(B)booklet
Expected occurrences = n·P(A)booklet
P(A only) = P(A) − P(A ∩ B)from the Venn
Traps
  • Mutually exclusive is not independent. If A and B can't both happen, knowing A happened tells you B didn't. That is dependence.
  • "A or B" in IB means inclusive: A, B, or both.
  • "Exactly one of A, B" is A ∪ B minus A ∩ B.
Shorthand
  • ME = "can't both". IND = "doesn't care".
  • Fill Venns from the middle out: overlap first, then the "only" regions, then outside.

SL 4.6, 4.11

Conditional probability and tree diagrams

"Given that B happened" means you throw away every outcome outside B. B becomes your new sample space, so P(B) becomes the denominator.

The idea

P(A | B) asks: of the times B happens, what fraction also have A? That is the overlap divided by B. In a two-way table, the condition tells you which row or column total to divide by.

Trees handle events in stages. Each branch carries a conditional probability, because the second stage is drawn knowing how the first went.

  • Multiply along a path. You need this and then that.
  • Add across paths. Any of these routes will do.

Without replacement, the second-stage numbers change: one fewer item in the bag, and one fewer of whatever was taken.

A neat check for independence: A and B are independent exactly when P(A | B) = P(A). The condition makes no difference.

Formulas

P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B)P(B)booklet
P(A ∩ B) = P(A)·P(B | A)multiply along
P(B) = P(A)P(B|A) + P(A′)P(B|A′)add across
Independent ⇔ P(A | B) = P(A)test
Traps
  • P(A | B) ≠ P(B | A). P(rain | clouds) is not P(clouds | rain).
  • Without replacement, P(2nd is red) is still r/n. Symmetry: any single draw is equally likely to be any item.
  • Don't divide by the grand total when the question says "given".
Shorthand
  • "Given" = new denominator.
  • Along ×, across +.

AHL 4.13

Bayes' theorem

Bayes reverses a conditional. The tree gives you P(evidence | cause). The question wants P(cause | evidence).

The idea

Draw the tree with the cause first (disease or not, machine A or B) and the evidence second (test positive, item defective). Then apply the conditional probability definition:

P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) ÷ P(B).

  • The numerator is one path: the cause you care about, followed by the evidence.
  • The denominator is every path that ends in the evidence. You get it by total probability.

That is all Bayes' theorem is. The booklet version just writes the denominator out in full.

The surprising part is base rates. For a rare condition, even an accurate test produces more false positives than true positives, simply because there are so many healthy people. Push the prevalence slider down in the model to see this.

Formulas

P(A | B) = P(A)P(B | A)P(A)P(B | A) + P(A′)P(B | A′)booklet
P(Ai | B) = P(Ai)P(B | Ai)Σ P(Aj)P(B | Aj)booklet

The second form handles three or more causes (three machines, three suppliers). Same logic: one path over all paths.

Traps
  • Sensitivity = P(+ | ill). Specificity = P(− | healthy). The false positive rate is 1 − specificity.
  • Ignoring the prior: P(ill | +) is not the test accuracy.
Shorthand
  • Bayes = one path ÷ all paths to the evidence.
  • Stuck? Imagine 1000 people and count.

AHL 1.10 · used in probability

Counting: permutations and combinations

When outcomes are equally likely, probability is a counting problem: favourable ÷ total. The only real question is whether order matters.

The idea

Arranging n different objects: n choices for the first slot, n − 1 for the second, and so on. That is n!.

Permutations nPr: fill r ordered slots from n objects. Stop the factorial after r terms.

Combinations nCr: choose a group of r where order doesn't matter. Each group was counted r! times as a permutation (once per ordering), so divide by r!.

Repeated letters: swapping identical letters gives the same word, so divide by the factorial of each repeat count.

Must be together: glue them into one block, arrange the blocks, then multiply by the arrangements inside the block.

At least one: use the complement. Total minus "none".

Formulas

nPr = n!(n − r)!booklet
nCr = n!r!(n − r)!booklet
Repeats: n!p! q! …identical items
Together: (n − k + 1)! × k!k items glued
Traps
  • Never count "at least one" as "pick one, then pick any". That double counts.
  • "Exactly 2 women" means the rest must be men. Multiply both choices.
Shorthand
  • P for Position (order matters). C for Choose (it doesn't).
  • nCr = nPr ÷ r!. Remove the orderings.

SL 4.7 · AHL 4.14

Discrete random variables

A random variable X turns each outcome into a number. "Discrete" means you can list its values. The table of values and probabilities is its whole description.

The idea

The probabilities in the table must add to 1. Most "find k" questions are just that equation.

E(X) is the long-run average. Weight each value by how often it occurs: Σ x·P(X = x). It is the balance point of the bar chart and need not be a value X can take.

A game is fair when the expected gain is 0. Remember to subtract the cost to play.

Variance (HL) measures spread: the average squared distance from the mean. The working form is E(X²) − [E(X)]². For E(X²), square the values but not the probabilities.

Linear transforms (HL): adding b shifts every value, so the mean moves but the spread doesn't. Multiplying by a stretches distances by a, so the variance scales by a².

Formulas

Σ P(X = x) = 1find k
E(X) = Σ x·P(X = x)booklet
Var(X) = E(X²) − [E(X)]²booklet
E(aX + b) = aE(X) + bbooklet
Var(aX + b) = a²Var(X)booklet
Traps
  • E(X²) ≠ [E(X)]². Compute E(X²) separately.
  • Var(−2X) = 4Var(X), not −2Var(X). Variance is never negative.
  • SD of aX + b is |a|·σ.
Shorthand
  • Shift moves the mean, not the spread.
  • Scale squares in the variance.

SL 4.8

Binomial distribution

Count the number of successes in a fixed number of identical, independent trials. X ~ B(n, p).

The idea

Check the conditions with BINS: Binary outcomes, Independent trials, Number of trials fixed, Same p each time.

Why the formula works: one particular sequence with r successes and n − r failures has probability pr(1 − p)n−r. There are nCr ways to choose which trials are the successes, and each way is equally likely. Multiply.

The mean np is intuitive: 20 trials at 30% gives 6 successes on average.

On the fx-CG50, Bcd adds up every value from a whole-number Lower to Upper. So the real skill is turning words like "fewer than" or "more than" into those bounds. The conversions in the Formulas drawer (press F) are the same logic, written as ≤ statements, which is how you show the step in your working.

On the TI-84, binomcdf only gives P(X ≤ x), always counting from 0. So the real skill is turning words like "fewer than" or "more than" into ≤ statements. The conversions in the Formulas drawer (press F) do exactly that, and writing them down is how you show the step in your working.

"Smallest n so that P(at least one) > 0.95": P(X ≥ 1) = 1 − (1 − p)n. Solve with logs or a GDC table, then round up.

Formulas

P(X = r) = nCr pr(1 − p)n − rbooklet
E(X) = npbooklet
Var(X) = np(1 − p)booklet

Convert to ≤ (whole numbers only)

P(X < k) = P(X ≤ k − 1)
P(X ≥ k) = 1 − P(X ≤ k − 1)
P(X > k) = 1 − P(X ≤ k)
P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = P(X ≤ b) − P(X ≤ a − 1)
Traps
  • "More than 3" starts at 4. "At least 3" starts at 3.
  • Without replacement from a small population is not binomial (p changes).
Shorthand
  • Strict sign? Step one integer inward. < 4 → Upper 3; > 4 → Lower 5.
  • At least one = 1 − none.

SL 4.9, 4.12

Normal distribution

The model for continuous measurements that cluster symmetrically around a mean: heights, masses, exam scores, errors. X ~ N(μ, σ²).

The idea

Probability is the area under the curve. The total area is 1, the curve is symmetric about μ, and mean = median = mode.

Because X is continuous, P(X = a) = 0. So P(X < a) and P(X ≤ a) are the same. No integer adjustments here, unlike the binomial.

Roughly 68% lies within 1σ of μ, 95% within 2σ, 99.7% within 3σ.

Standardising: z = (x − μ)/σ counts how many standard deviations x is from the mean. Then Z ~ N(0, 1).

You need z whenever μ or σ is unknown:

  1. Turn the probability into a z-value with the inverse normal (μ = 0, σ = 1).
  2. Write (x − μ)/σ = z.
  3. Solve for the unknown. With two unknowns you get two equations: solve simultaneously.

Formulas and calculator

Traps
  • N(50, 16) means σ² = 16, so σ = 4. The calculator wants σ.
  • Casio asks for σ before μ.
  • The TI asks for μ before σ (the reverse of Casio).
  • InvN's Tail must match the inequality. P(X > k) = 0.1 → Tail Right, Area 0.1.
  • invNorm uses the area to the left. P(X > k) = 0.1 → invNorm(0.9, μ, σ).
  • When finding σ or μ, get z with σ = 1 and μ = 0, not with the real parameters.
Shorthand
  • Tail follows the sign: > is Right, < is Left.
  • invNorm thinks left: for >, feed it 1 − area.
  • z = "how many σ from μ".
  • Always sketch the curve and shade it before pressing buttons.

  2. One unknown: rearrange (x − μ)/σ = z by hand.
  3. Two unknowns: APPS → PlySmlt2 → 2:SIMULTANEOUS EQN SOLVER, 2 equations, 2 unknowns. Enter rows 1 z₁ | a and 1 z₂ | b for μ + zσ = x, then SOLVE (GRAPH).

No PlySmlt2? Subtract the equations: σ = (b − a)/(z₂ − z₁), then μ = a − z₁σ.

AHL 4.14

Continuous random variables

A probability density function f(x) describes a continuous variable. Probabilities are areas, so every question is an integral.

The idea

f(x) is not a probability. It is a density, and can exceed 1. Probability is the area under f between two values.

For f to be a valid pdf, f(x) ≥ 0 everywhere and the total area is 1. "Find k" questions use that second condition.

The discrete formulas carry over with Σ replaced by ∫ and P(X = x) replaced by f(x) dx.

  • Mean: the balance point, ∫ x f(x) dx.
  • Median m: the vertical line splitting the area in half, ∫ f from the lower end to m = 0.5.
  • Mode: where f is highest. Check f′(x) = 0 and the endpoints.

A right-skewed shape pulls the mean further into the tail than the median. Watch the three lines in the model.

Formulas

∫ f(x) dx = 1 over the domainfind k
P(a < X < b) = ∫ab f(x) dxbooklet
E(X) = ∫ x f(x) dxbooklet
Var(X) = ∫ x² f(x) dx − μ²booklet
∫lowm f(x) dx = 0.5median
Traps
  • The mode can sit at an endpoint, where f′(x) ≠ 0.
  • When solving for the median, keep only the root inside the domain.
  • For piecewise f, split each integral at the joins.
Shorthand
  • Area = probability. Balance = mean. Half-area = median. Peak = mode.

Mixed drill

Questions from every topic, in random order. This is closest to a real paper, because part of the skill is recognising which tool a question wants before you start.

Exam habits that save marks

  • Write the distribution down first, e.g. "X ~ B(20, 0.3)" or "X ~ N(170, 8²)". It earns method marks and catches variance/SD mix-ups.
  • Write the probability statement before the number: "P(X ≥ 4) = 1 − P(X ≤ 3) = …".
  • Give answers to 3 significant figures unless told otherwise. Keep full GDC precision in intermediate steps.
  • Sketch every normal question. A shaded sketch takes 10 seconds and catches most tail errors.
